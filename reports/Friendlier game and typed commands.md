# Show players the goal, reshape typing

**Short answer.** The game's biggest gap for a new player is not wording or layout. Those have had three fix passes. The gap is direction: nothing on screen always says what you are aiming at or what to do next. The four best ways to make the game friendlier and more fun, in order, are: (1) an always-visible "your aim, and what needs you now" strip; (2) steering a first-time player into the guided lesson and making it shorter; (3) letting the player see trouble coming before they press End Turn; (4) giving each marshal more voice at the moments the player decides. On typing, my verdict is **keep it, but give it a different job**. Clicking is faster for a plain order. Typing earns its place for asking questions, for one-line orders to several armies, and for talking to Berthier and the marshals. The outside evidence is thin. It comes mostly from developer statements, reviews and analogy. Nobody has measured a typed bar in a mouse-first strategy game. Where I say "measured", I mean it. Where I say "opinion", it is opinion.

## What the first playtest and the records say

The developer's first playtest ended with "everything feels convoluted and opaque and disconnected". Every specific complaint from it is marked fixed in the game's records. Fresh reviewers who had never seen the game were later shown only screenshots at the developer's own screen setup. After fixes, three of those rounds passed with 18 of 18 answers right. The problems they kept finding were bare numbers with no word beside them, jargon, buttons that do not say what the next press does, and map symbols with no key. That is a vocabulary problem, and it is being closed.

What the records do not show is any fix for "disconnected". I checked two facts in the code. The guided lesson is only an optional row on the main menu. The campaign's goal is told only if the player types a question for it. A single war's aim shows in the war panel's detail lines. Nothing always on screen shows the campaign aim or the next step. The game's own scoring says first contact reads 8.0 to 8.5 out of 10, and that score comes from typed questions the game answers well. It measures what happens if you ask. A player who never asks never hears it.

The outside complaints match. Reviewers of Europa Universalis V's launch and Civilization VII's launch complained of missing information, missed events, and a screen that "can't separate what's a bother and what's important" ([Aftermath](https://aftermath.site/civilization-vii-review/); [Tobold](https://tobolds.blogspot.com/2025/11/europa-universalis-v-first-impressions.html)). They did not mainly complain that the lesson was too short.

## Four ways to make the game friendlier and more fun, ranked

| Rank | Change | Size | Mostly builds on |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | An always-visible aim and "needs you now" strip | Medium | Berthier's goal answer, the Congress clock line, the morning briefing |
| 2 | Steer new players into a shorter guided lesson | Small to medium | The School of War and the first-open lines |
| 3 | See trouble coming before End Turn | Medium | The alerts queue, the enemy replay, the "what if" preview |
| 4 | More marshal voice where the player decides | Medium | Objections, the pecking order, battle lines |

### 1. Put the aim and the next step on screen, always

**What to change.** Add one slim strip, always visible, with two parts. The first part is the aim in plain words: what winning looks like now and how far along you are (for example, the tally the game already builds for the Congress of Paris, which reads like "43 of 45 titled"). The second part is a short "needs you now" list of two or three things, each with the reason it matters. Clicking an item opens the right screen. Today Berthier says all this only when typed to. The same text, pushed to the screen, costs little. Separately, there is a **new** idea to label as new: a short list of country-specific goals for the next few turns. For France in 1805 this could be "take the road to Vienna" or "keep Ney and Davout loyal". It would be authored, not generated. Each goal must name who owns it, which slice lands it, and a test, under the project's own rule on promised work.

**Why.** The strongest outside example is Victoria 3's journal. It is one place for objectives, lessons and explanations. Entries react to the player's country and come in sequence ([Paradox dev diary](https://www.paradoxinteractive.com/games/victoria-3/news/dev-diary-51-tutorials)). Its developers later said, per a search summary I could not open in full, that they should explain the "why" of each entry more. Sid Meier's design talk says strategy games work on short, medium and long goals at once ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/gdc-2012-sid-meier-on-how-to-see-games-as-sets-of-interesting-decisions)). Short, country-specific goal sets were judged more fun than long generic ones in Europa Universalis IV, though some players call goal trees railroading ([Paradox diary](https://admin-forum.paradoxplaza.com/forum/developer-diary/eu4-development-diary-19th-of-march-2019.1161211)). That is why the goals should be short, optional, and never force a path. This is design talk and reviews, not measurement.

**How we would know.** Show three new reviewers only screenshots of turn one, with no typing allowed. Ask "what are you trying to do?" and "what would you do next?". The bar is that all three answer right. Then watch a stranger play ten turns and count how often they type "what now".

### 2. Steer new players into a short guided lesson

**What to change.** On first launch with no save, offer the School of War as the highlighted first choice, with a plain line saying it takes about this long and can be skipped. Cut its length only if the developer decides to. It runs about twenty cards today. Keep the lesson on the game's own screens, as it already is.

**Why.** Crusader Kings III's own numbers are the best outside evidence. Its team found that finishing the tutorial barely predicted who kept playing. They cut a 67-box lesson to about a third and moved teaching into events and tooltips ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/deep-dive-refreshing-the-crusader-kings-iii-tutorial-mode-through-optimized-ux)). No result number was published. A 2012 University of Washington experiment on more than 45,000 players found tutorials raised play time by up to 29 percent in the most complex game and made no real difference in simple ones ([project page](https://grail.cs.washington.edu/projects/game-abtesting/chi2012/)). Those games were not grand strategy, so it is only a hint. On Steam, small strategy games lose most players across a tutorial sequence ([Steam stats](https://steamcommunity.com/stats/421260/achievements)), though those are snapshots of unknown date.

**How we would know.** Measure whether players later use what the lesson taught, such as the map key or the ledger. Do not measure whether they finished it. The Crusader Kings result is the warning. We do not know whether the developer's first player used the lesson at all.

### 3. Let the player see trouble coming before End Turn

**What to change.** Two parts. First, a short check when the player presses End Turn: "2 corps idle, 1 letter unanswered, Bavaria's army is two roads from Munich." The game already has an idle-corps key and a priority order for popups. This check turns them into one visible line. Second, make the free "what if Ney attacks Mack" odds a button on the attack card, since today it is taught as a typed line. A **new** and larger idea: mark your provinces that enemy armies in sight could reach next turn, using only what the player can already see, so the fog stays honest.

**Why.** Slay the Spire's designers say showing each enemy's next move is what made the game work, and clicking each enemy to check would be tedious. Into the Breach's designers built the whole game on showing every enemy attack first ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/road-to-the-igf-subset-games-i-into-the-breach-i-)). These are designer statements, not measurements. Alert noise and missed events drew complaints at both recent big launches. Europa Universalis V's patch added alert muting, a clearer call-to-war notice and pinned filters ([FinalBoss.io](https://finalboss.io/europa-universalis-5-rossbach-1-1-tackles-ui), a secondary source). The game's own score-finish record says one worst-case End Turn raised three blocking pop-ups. The enemy-move replay already built answers the "after" half.

**How we would know.** Count turns where the player ended the turn and then undid it, or lost a province they did not know was threatened. Both should fall.

### 4. Give marshals more voice at the moment of decision

**What to change.** The game's own review found four aggressive marshals share one objection line, and that the middle of the marshal drama is thin, with grievances that "run their course" with no consequence named. Write two or three lines per personality per trigger, and make the reply name the cause and the lever: "Ney is angry about Mack's glory. Trust or Insist?". Show the pecking order and grudges in the objection card itself.

**Why.** Attachment to named subordinates comes from seeing personality at the moment of play, plus real stakes. Battle Brothers is the warning: fighters with mechanics but little expressed personality drew less attachment ([PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/battle-brothers-is-turn-based-mount-blade-meets-xcom)). Total War: Three Kingdoms turns officers' satisfaction into visible drama such as defection ([CGM](https://www.cgmagonline.com/reviews/total-war-three-kingdoms-review/)). For disobedience, my reading of the sources is that it delights when the cause is readable and the player holds a lever, and infuriates when it adds friction with no reason. That is inference. No source tests it. Self-determination research, measured in four studies, links enjoyment to feeling in control and competent ([Ryan, Rigby, Przybylski 2006](https://pure.ewha.ac.kr/en/publications/the-motivational-pull-of-video-games-a-self-determination-theory-/)).

**How we would know.** Reviewers shown two objections should be able to tell which marshal spoke without the name. Count repeated lines across a ten-turn game: the aim is none within a marshal's last three.

## Part 2: keep the typed line, but change its job

**Verdict: keep it, but give it a different job.** Make it the place to ask and to give one-line group orders, not the main way to give a single order. I would not cut it. Cutting is the developer's decision, and the evidence does not push that way.

**Evidence against typing as the main road.** The game's own test of 28 typed lines found every order has a button and a click is faster. I could not find the 28 lines themselves in the repository, so that result cannot be re-checked. Parser games lost the mainstream because the box promises "anything" but takes only what the author built, so players guess the words ([Emily Short](https://emshort.blog/2010/06/07/so-do-we-need-this-parser-thing-anyway/); [Maniac Mansion's interface](https://www.pcgamer.com/how-maniac-mansions-verb-object-interface-revolutionised-adventure-games)). Even the best-received voice-command strategy games ship another way to play, and reviewers call voice a mood-maker, not the fastest input ([Kotaku on EndWar](https://kotaku.com/tom-clancys-endwar-review-vocal-annihilation-5086994)). A developer of Radio General measured about 70 percent accuracy out of the box and called that too low ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/audio/the-journey-to-speech-recognition-and-how-to-do-it-yourself)). Without an API key, the shipped game uses the built-in parser. On the game's own twenty-order blind test it read 11 of 20 as meant on one tree and 19 of 20 on a later one, and a refusal costs nothing. So some strangers' phrasings will still fail.

**Evidence for.** The words typing is good at are things buttons do badly. Typing in the same idiom as the output feels more engaging than menus (Short's opinion, same link). Typing earned praise in Duskers, where drone commands can be chained in one line ([GamingOnLinux](https://www.gamingonlinux.com/2016/05/duskers-leaves-early-access-heres-a-review/page=1/)). It earned praise in Cryptmaster, though a reviewer spent minutes hunting for words with weak cues ([Press Start](https://press-start.com.au/reviews/pc-reviews/2024/05/10/cryptmaster-review-words-are-weapons/)). The cure for guessing is to show what is possible: suggestions, autocomplete, teaching errors. Command palettes work this way: you type a fragment you half remember and pick from a visible list ([Rob Dodson](https://robdodson.me/command-palettes/)). The game already has this. The empty line offers ready-made questions, Tab completes names, and typos get a "did you mean". A 1985 comparison of 76 users found that how well an interface was built mattered more than whether it used commands or menus ([abstract](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/user-performance-with-command-menu-and-iconic-interfaces/812025262521712640-18187)). That supports fixing the typed line over cutting it. It was an old study, read from its abstract only.

**How to make it fun, not a chore.** In order of how much I would do first:

First, **close the silent drops.** Four known cases ignore part of a long order and spend nothing. "Murat, march to Lorraine then attack Mack and charge Mack" quietly drops the third order. The game's own rule is that every input gets an answer. Every order that is partly understood should read back what it took and what it left. Second, **add the answer players most expect and cannot get.** "Who threatens me?" works only for a named province. The notes say it is recommended and has no owner or landing slice, which the project's own rule requires before work continues. Third, **let the player plead in their own words.** When a marshal objects, the command line is off and there are three buttons. Allow a typed line such as "Ney, trust me, this once", read by the same words-only parser and mapped to the same three buttons, with the same outcome. This is **new**. The parser never decides anything. It reads the words and picks one of the three choices. Fourth, **let marshals answer questions in their own voice.** "Ney, what do you think of attacking Mack?" could return an authored line shaped by personality and the current relations. This is **new**. The answer is a reading of existing numbers, so it fits the rule that the language model never affects outcomes. Fifth, **tell the player what the line is for.** The boot hint should say "ask me anything, or give a group order in one line", not just list verbs. Sixth, **count it.** Record locally how often orders are typed against clicked. Nobody has published usage numbers for optional command bars, so the developer's own data is the missing evidence.

The fun in recent free-text games came from a character reacting to what you said. Those games used a language model to write replies. Suck Up! sits at 62 percent positive of 233 reviews ([Steam](https://store.steampowered.com/app/2726370/Suck_Up/)), and Whispers from the Star at about 82 percent of 1,618 ([SteamDB](https://steamdb.info/app/3730100/graphs)). This game writes its replies by hand instead, so it cannot lean on those numbers. It can reach the same feeling with authored lines, which are also safer.

## How strong this evidence is, and what is missing

**Measured:** the Crusader Kings tutorial finding (telemetry, but no result numbers published), the 2012 tutorial experiment (not grand strategy), the Steam scores quoted, the Radio General accuracy figure, and the game's own blind readings and parser tests. **Opinion or design talk:** the Slay the Spire and Into the Breach statements, all the Paradox goal and journal material, the marshal-attachment reasoning, and every "rule" about when disobedience is fun.

**Gaps.** There is no study of a typed bar in a mouse-first strategy game. There is no controlled test of previews, undo, end-turn checks, or goal strips in any strategy game. There is no retention number for Crusader Kings III, Victoria 3, or any Napoleonic game. I found no research on command consoles in Paradox games or Dwarf Fortress. Some pages were read only as search summaries, and I marked those where I used them. The 28-line test and whether it used the keyless or the model-backed parser cannot be confirmed from the records. I could not confirm whether the first player took the guided lesson, or what parser mode his first play used. Nobody has re-scored the client's look and feel since early October. No source separates a model that only reads words from one that writes content, so I cannot say how players would take an "AI" label here. A survey of about 3,800 Steam players found 31 percent negative on AI disclosure in general ([GameDiscoverCo](https://newsletter.gamediscover.co/p/what-do-steam-fans-really-think-about)), and the game's rule keeps the model off by default.

## Conclusion

The developer's three fix passes made the game readable. What remains is making it feel like one game with a direction. The cheapest big win is moving what Berthier already says when asked onto the screen, so the typed line stops being the only way to learn the aim. That also frees typing to do what it does best. Typing is worth keeping because asking and addressing several armies are things a button handles badly. It is not worth keeping as the way to move one corps. If the developer agrees, the next step is a stranger test with typing switched off, then the same test with the aim strip on, so the result is measured and not argued.
