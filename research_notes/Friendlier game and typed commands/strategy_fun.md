# What makes turn-based strategy fun and hard to put down, with special attention to games where the player commands subordinates with their own will (notes as of 19 Nov 2026)

Method note: roughly 20 searches and 3 page fetches. Many results were snippets, not full texts. Items marked (opinion) are blogs, forums or reviews. Items marked (design statement) are from designers. Items marked (measured) come from surveys or review counts. No published number was found for most questions about pacing, session length or drop-off; those are listed under Gaps. Nothing here comes from the developer's own project.

## Goals and the "one more turn" loop (overlapping goals, mission trees, legacy paths, sandboxes without goals)

### Takeaway
Practitioners agree that the loop is overlapping time scales: something is always one or two turns from paying off. Authored goals (EU4 missions) are credited with the addictiveness and also attacked as railroading. Civ VII's goal structure (legacy paths, age transitions) divided players. No peer-reviewed study of "one more turn" was found.

### Cited Findings
- Sid Meier (GDC 2012, design statement): Civ works on "short, medium, and long-term levels at once"; a wonder is slow with big long-term payoff, a chariot is quick and minor. This is the clearest design statement of overlapping goals. — [Game Developer, GDC 2012 write-up](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/gdc-2012-sid-meier-on-how-to-see-games-as-sets-of-interesting-decisions)
- A blogger (opinion) describes 4X as "several things happening at once," where you are "frequently just a turn or two away from researching a new technology or conquering a city." — [Rampant Coyote](https://rampantgames.com/blog/?p=6222)
- Aftermath's Nathan Grayson (opinion) argues "one more turn" is a ploy: each click makes numbers go up or something else change, like a slot machine, and "they've all got that pull, and it's not because the game's good." — [Aftermath podcast, Civ VII "one more turn"](https://aftermath.site/aftermath-hours-podcast-civilization-vii-one-more-turn)
- A PCGamesN writer with ADHD (anecdote) describes the pull as "a huge, unguessable buffet of rewards ahead of you, waiting to ripen." — [PCGamesN](https://www.pcgamesn.com/adhd-strategy-gaming)
- EU4 missions (Paradox dev diary, 19 Mar 2019, design statement): generic missions were harder and slower to design and less fun than shorter country-specific trees; Paradox called the redesign a success in community reception while admitting some players prefer the old system. — [EU4 dev diary](https://admin-forum.paradoxplaza.com/forum/developer-diary/eu4-development-diary-19th-of-march-2019.1161211)
- A forum poster (opinion) says one aim of earlier EU3 missions was to tell the player what to do and keep nations near historical borders. — [Paradox forum](https://admin-forum.paradoxplaza.com/forum/threads/the-game-is-starting-to-feel-more-like-use-the-mission-tree.1534685/page-5)
- A Substack review (opinion) credits the mission tree's milestone bonuses for EU4's addictiveness. — [Scanlines](https://scanlines.substack.com/p/the-game-where-you-carve-up-the-world)
- Player criticism (opinion, forums): linear trees railroad, make every playthrough of a nation the same, and "one-click" rewards undercut the sandbox. — [Paradox forum thread](https://admin-forum.paradoxplaza.com/forum/threads/missions-are-too-linear-discourage-variety-and-encourage-playing-each-faction-the-same-way-each-time.1459084)
- Civ VII (measured, 2025): Steam reviews started "Mostly Negative", sat near 41% positive from about 2,600 reviews at 11 hours, and later about 50% from nearly 8,000; one aggregator lists 48% of 45,387 but its date is unconfirmed. Age transitions drew the most criticism (an academic analysis says they reset the map and impose era goals and civ switching); legacy paths split opinion: "narrow, restrictive" versus "incentivises a wider variety of strategies." Developers acknowledged the transitions were not well received. UI was the other frequent complaint. — [search digest of Steam reviews and DiGRA paper](https://dl.digra.org/index.php/dl/article/download/3093/3077/3136); [Notebookcheck on launch backlash](https://www.notebookcheck.net/Civilization-VII-s-rough-launch-sparks-backlash-developers-pledge-improvements.958568.0.html); [Vaporlens summary](https://vaporlens.app/app/1295660/sid_meiers_civilization_vii)
- Old World (2021 reviews, opinion): billed as Civilization with Crusader Kings events and characters, with a mortal ruler. PC Gamer 87; OpenCritic aggregate 6.5/10; complaints were a brutal learning curve and a poor tutorial. — [PC Gamer](https://pcgamer.com/old-world-review); [OpenCritic](https://opencritic.com/game/11628/-); [Turn Based Lovers](https://turnbasedlovers.com/review/old-world-review)
- Quantic Foundry (measured, 2024 report on 1.7 million surveys, via an Italian outlet): interest in deep strategy declined over nine years while most other motivations were stable; Quantic could not identify a cause. — [Multiplayer.it](https://multiplayer.it/notizie/giocatori-vogliono-sempre-meno-preoccuparsi-strategia-videogiochi.html)

### Inferences
- Overlapping horizons (this turn, in 3 to 5 turns, campaign-long) are the repeated explanation. Mission trees add authored mid-term goals but cost sandbox freedom; the mid-way fix suggested by the EU4 evidence is short, nation-specific goal sets rather than long generic or linear ones.
- Civ VII suggests that resetting goals or state at a transition costs momentum.
- Inference: the declining appetite for deep strategy implies a campaign that only rewards mastery of rules will reach fewer players.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study of the "one more turn" effect; the Zeigarnik-effect claim appears only in an uncited blog and is not reported here.
- No found evidence on what happens to goal-less sandboxes beyond forum opinion; no retention data for mission trees.
- Old World "ambitions" mechanics and Total War campaign objectives were not retrieved. Their reception is unresearched here.

## "Interesting decisions", optimizing the fun out, and what makes a decision meaningful

### Takeaway
Meier's checklist (tradeoffs, situational, persistent, informed, with feedback) is the most-cited practitioner standard. Johnson's "players will optimize the fun out of a game" is widely agreed but its exact original wording is unverified.

### Cited Findings
- Meier (GDC 2012): "Games are a series of interesting decisions." Not interesting: always picking the first of three options, or random choice. Interesting decisions have tradeoffs, depend on game state, express playstyle, persist, carry risk versus reward, and trade short term against long term. — [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/gdc-2012-sid-meier-on-how-to-see-games-as-sets-of-interesting-decisions)
- Meier on information: err "on the side of providing the player with too much information"; an unexpected result from a familiar input is "nothing more disconcerting." — same source.
- Meier on feedback: "The worst thing you can do is just move on" after a decision; even a sound effect helps, and feedback should reinforce the player's fantasy. — same source.
- Meier on pacing: back-to-back complex decisions leave players feeling "out of control"; very simple decisions in slow moments bore them. He estimates "probably a third of the things that we try, if not more," get cut. — same source.
- Meier: what is interesting once is not interesting ten times. — [Escapist, GDC 2012](https://www.escapistmagazine.com/v2/gdc-2012-sid-meier-sees-interesting-decisions-even-in-rhythm-games/)
- "Players will optimize the fun out of a game" is attributed to Soren Johnson; one 2025 blog cites his 2011 "Water Finds a Crack" journal entry as the source, but the original wording could not be opened. — [TheGamer](https://www.thegamer.com/players-optimize-fun-out-of-games); [PhD20 blog](https://phd20-com.pages.dev/blog/dont-optimize-the-fun-out-of-your-game)
- Self-determination research (measured, 2006): across four studies perceived autonomy and competence predicted enjoyment, preference and well-being change; relatedness also predicted enjoyment and future play. Intuitive controls and immersion were tied to these needs. — [Ryan, Rigby, Przybylski 2006 abstract](https://pure.ewha.ac.kr/en/publications/the-motivational-pull-of-video-games-a-self-determination-theory-/); [Przybylski, Rigby, Ryan 2010 model](https://digitalcommons.butler.edu/ccom_papers/146)

### Inferences
- A decision feels opaque when its information or its consequence is hidden; Meier's remedy is more information and always showing a response.
- Defiance by a marshal is only an "interesting decision" if the player could have predicted its likelihood and had a lever to change it. This follows from Meier's "informed" and "persistent" criteria; no source tests it for defiance specifically.

### Gaps
- The Johnson original wording was not verified. Evidence that decisions "feel meaningful" is design opinion; no controlled study found.

## Feedback and reward: visible consequence, celebration, end-of-turn summaries, chronicles

### Takeaway
Feedback evidence is design-statement level: respond to every decision and reinforce the fantasy. No measured data on "juice" or end-of-turn summaries in map strategy was found.

### Cited Findings
- Meier: the response to a decision should reinforce the player's fantasy via sound, text or visuals (see above). — [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/gdc-2012-sid-meier-on-how-to-see-games-as-sets-of-interesting-decisions)
- Aftermath (opinion) says each "next turn" click produces visible number changes; critics call this manipulation if nothing deeper sits behind it. — [Aftermath](https://aftermath.site/aftermath-hours-podcast-civilization-vii-one-more-turn)
- Reviews of Crusader Kings III (opinion) praise emergent arcs and one says some arcs "seem too perfect" — the log of what happened is where players find the story. — [PC Gamer CK3 review](https://pcgamer.com/uk/crusader-kings-3-review); [Shacknews](https://shacknews.com/article/120077/crusader-kings-3-review-your-noble-story-eternally-unfolding)

### Inferences
- A chronicle of marshals' deeds and grudges is likely to be valued, but this is not supported by a measured source.

### Gaps
- No numbers on juice, celebration or end-of-turn report effects were found. Meier's talk is the strongest source.

## Pacing: turn length, AI waits, interruptions, early wins, micromanagement

### Takeaway
No measured pacing data (session length, drop-off, time-to-first-win) was found. The available evidence is review commentary that micromanagement and interface overload turn off players.

### Cited Findings
- March of the Eagles (2013 reviews, opinion): Gaming Shogun (3/5) called it overwhelming, with constant micromanagement of finances, diplomacy, research and troops; GameSpot (7/10) noted it dropped many Paradox systems yet kept enough depth for multiplayer to redeem it; PC Gamer scored 80, praising battles and low price; Softpedia 8/10 praised tutorials and hints. — [Gaming Shogun](https://gamingshogun.com/?p=37655); [GameSpot](https://www.gamespot.com/games/march-of-the-eagles/reviews/); [PC Gamer](https://pcgamer.com/march-of-the-eagles-review); [Softpedia](https://www.softpedia.com/reviews/games/pc/March-of-the-Eagles-Review-331811.shtml)
- Meier on pacing decisions (see previous section). — [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/gdc-2012-sid-meier-on-how-to-see-games-as-sets-of-interesting-decisions)
- Total War: Three Kingdoms and Old World reviews flag interface overload and steep learning curves (see their sections).

### Inferences
- Complaints of micromanagement and opacity are common to every game in this set; this matches the developer's first-playtest verdict.

### Gaps
- No found published data on session length, early-game drop-off, or AI waiting time. Not available from these sources.

## Character and story as fun: named characters with personalities, grudges, memory

### Takeaway
Reviews and designer statements agree that named, persistent, mortal characters with memory generate personal stories. Evidence is qualitative.

### Cited Findings
- Crusader Kings III (Paradox diary, design statement): stress balances personality traits against each other; mental breaks can change a character's traits; diary #58 frames the appeal as personifying many characters with roleplay opportunities. A player comment says the AI handles stress badly. — [CK3 diary 31](https://admin-forum.paradoxplaza.com/forum/developer-diary/ck3-dev-diary-31-a-stressful-situation.1399764/page-2); [CK3 diary 58](https://forum.paradoxplaza.com/forum/threads/ck3-dev-diary-58-stre-ss-tching-the-traits.1472092)
- Nemesis system: design director Michael de Plater asked at DICE 2015 "How can we make narrative out of the gameplay?"; the team used self-determination theory (competence, autonomy, relatedness) as a reference; Monolith's GDC 2018 talk aimed to maximize positive and negative emotions toward a nemesis. De Plater's postmortem says the system had more layers than shipped and the team had no benchmark proving it would work. — [Self-determination theory site on Gamasutra article](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/gamasutra/); [GDC 2018 listing](https://gdconf.com/news/attend-gdc-2018-behind-scenes-look-shadow-wars-nemesis-system); [GamesRadar](https://gamesradar.com/middle-earth-shadow-of-mordors-nemesis-system-was-patented-by-warner-bros)
- XCOM (opinion): Jake Solomon says permadeath makes players attached; a 2019 blog (Tiago Costa) lists naming, progression, shared hardship, scarcity (10 to 15 soldiers, about 6 advanced, his estimate) and random outcomes as causes; a Killscreen essay says attachment comes from naming and permadeath, not shown socializing. — [PCGamesN interview](https://pcgamesn.com/xcom-2/xcom-jake-solomon-interview); [Game Developer essay](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/why-do-we-get-so-attached-to-our-soldiers-in-xcom-); [Killscreen](https://www.killscreen.com/how-xcom-taught-me-i-dont-matter/)
- Battle Brothers (PC Gamer, opinion): victories and losses feel personal, but fighters' personalities are under-expressed and covered faces dampened attachment. — [PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/battle-brothers-is-turn-based-mount-blade-meets-xcom)
- Total War: Three Kingdoms (2019 reviews, opinion): relationships feed satisfaction and battle performance; friends form, rivals may fight or betray over slights; unhappy officers can leave with their region and trigger civil war; CGM says tracking needs and relationships "creates another layer of strategy"; reviewers also complain of an overloaded interface. PC Gamer 78. — [CGM](https://www.cgmagonline.com/reviews/total-war-three-kingdoms-review/); [PC Gamer](https://pcgamer.com/total-war-three-kingdoms-review); [GamesRadar round-up](https://gamesradar.com/total-war-three-kingdoms-review-round-up); [Total War wiki](https://totalwar.fandom.com/wiki/Satisfaction)

### Inferences
- Attachment needs expression of personality at the moment of play (a face, a voice, a line), time, and stakes (loss). Battle Brothers shows that mechanics without expression underdeliver.
- Satisfaction/loyalty meters that cause visible, dramatic consequences (civil war, defection) are how Three Kingdoms turns character into drama.

### Gaps
- No measured study of character attachment. Football Manager was not researched. Mount & Blade lords not researched.

## Indirect command: when disobedience delights or infuriates, and design rules

### Takeaway
Little direct evidence was found on player response to disobedience as such. What exists: indirect-control games (Majesty, Radio General, Radio Commander) are praised for tension and immersion from limited control and faulted for clumsy interfaces and scripting, not for subordinates' will. Treat the design rules below as inference.

### Cited Findings
- Majesty (reviews, opinion): heroes decide on their own what to do and when; the player steers with monetary incentives (bounties); Majesty 2 added "Incentive Flags" to make indirect control "smoother and better"; veteran heroes proudly ignore rewards that do not fit their expectations; a later game, Crown of Greed, gives "influence without being able to control everything." — [DroidGamers](https://www.droidgamers.com/?p=20177); [GameSpot Majesty 2 Q&A](https://www.gamespot.com/articles/majesty-2-the-fantasy-kingdom-sim-updated-qanda/1100-6192803/); [Digitec on Crown of Greed](https://www.digitec.ch/en/page/gold-instead-of-obedience-crown-of-greed-tested-42293)
- Radio General: Steam "Very Positive" in one snapshot (512 reviews); you cannot see units and speak orders; the map does not auto-update and radio is sometimes lost, which Common Sense Education frames as deliberate; the player hopes standing orders and units' on-the-ground adaptation suffice. — [Steam](https://store.steampowered.com/app/1011610/); [Common Sense Education](https://www.commonsense.org/education/reviews/radio-general)
- Radio Commander: Vice says units do not get confused by contradictory orders (softening the premise) and that clumsy control becomes annoying with more units; GameGrin credits radio-only information for personal stake; a Steam summary lists overly scripted missions and bugs. — [Vice](https://www.vice.com/en/article/radio-commander-review/); [GameGrin](https://www.gamegrin.com/reviews/radio-commander-review); [Vaporlens](https://vaporlens.app/app/871530/radio_commander_vietnam_64)
- Matrix-family wargames (forum, not verified for Command Ops 2): with orders delay set to "realistic", reissuing orders after contact is "a pain"; waypoint adjustments carry no delay. A Flashpoint Campaigns dev log says readiness of HQs and subordinates affects the command cycle. — [Matrix forum](https://forums.matrixgames.com/viewtopic.php?p=817829); [Flashpoint dev log](https://www.matrixgames.com/news/flashpoint-campaigns-southern-storm-dev-log-2)
- CK3 stress and Three Kingdoms satisfaction are the nearest mainstream analogues of subordinate will (see Character section).

### Inferences
- Rule: delight tends to come when the cause is legible (a trait, a grievance, a reading), the player holds a lever (gold in Majesty, trust and satisfaction in Three Kingdoms) and the consequence is dramatic and paid off. This pattern is consistent across the sources but no source tests it.
- Rule: the fault players name is interface and information friction, not agency; so defiance that adds friction without a readable reason is the riskiest design.
- Rule: standing orders plus delegated adaptation (Radio General) turns subordinates' will into a skill to plan around rather than a random penalty.

### Gaps
- No found player data on when disobedience was "infuriating": Command Ops 2, Scourge of War, Take Command, HistWar: Les Grognards, Campaigns on the Danube, King of Dragon Pass and Mount & Blade lords yielded no usable sources. Not found.
- No numbers on how rare defiance should be.

## Napoleonic and operational strategy: praise and fault

### Takeaway
Napoleonic titles are praised for battles and spectacle and faulted for AI, short campaigns and lost depth. No source explaining March of the Eagles' sales was found.

### Cited Findings
- Napoleon: Total War (2010 reviews, opinion): GameSpot called it improved but less revolutionary; GamesRadar said it was very good but had slight historical inaccuracies and "somewhat stupid AI"; Armchair General faulted the short campaign map and limited playable countries and praised replay value and graphics. — [GameSpot](https://www.gamespot.com/reviews/napoleon-total-war-review/1900-6252611/); [GamesRadar](https://gamesradar.com/napoleon-total-war-review); [Armchair General](https://armchairgeneral.com/?p=6318)
- March of the Eagles (2013): critics split between its accessibility and the loss of Paradox economy, trade and population depth: The Koalition called it "strategic mediocrity" and hoped its ideas would reappear in EU IV. — [The Koalition](https://thekoalition.com/?p=69793); [GameSpot](https://www.gamespot.com/games/march-of-the-eagles/reviews/)

### Inferences
- The shared fault line is depth against clarity: removing systems did not satisfy fans, and leaving micromanagement in overwhelmed others.

### Gaps
- March of the Eagles' commercial underperformance was not confirmed; no sales data found. Victory and Glory: Napoleon, Ultimate General and Unity of Command were not researched; EU Napoleonic era also not covered.

## Player-motivation research (Quantic Foundry, self-determination theory)

### Takeaway
Quantic groups Strategy and Challenge under Mastery; its strategy fans cite EU4, CK2, Civ, XCOM. SDT's three needs predict enjoyment and continued play.

### Cited Findings
- Quantic (measured, own surveys): Mastery comprises Challenge (overcoming difficult challenges) and Strategy (careful decisions, strategic thinking); frequently mentioned Strategy titles include Europa Universalis 4, Crusader Kings 2, Civilization, XCOM, Fire Emblem. Challenge is the only motivation whose score rises again after age 45. — [Quantic Foundry GDC 2019 talk](https://quanticfoundry.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/03/Quantic-Foundry-GDC-2019-Talk.pdf); [Gamer Motivation Model Reference](https://quanticfoundry.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/03/Gamer-Motivation-Model-Reference-Updated.pdf)
- SDT: autonomy, competence and relatedness independently predict enjoyment and future play (2006); the 2010 model says appeal and well-being effects rest on satisfying these needs. PENS is the questionnaire operationalising them. — [Ryan et al. 2006](https://pure.ewha.ac.kr/en/publications/the-motivational-pull-of-video-games-a-self-determination-theory-/); [2010 model](https://digitalcommons.butler.edu/ccom_papers/146)
- Nemesis designers explicitly used SDT as a design reference (see Character section). — [SDT site](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/gamasutra/)

### Inferences
- Applied to subordinate-will games: competence = readable cause and effect; autonomy = meaningful levers; relatedness = bonds with marshals. Defiance threatens competence unless readable, and autonomy if the player has no lever. This mapping is inference.

### Gaps
- Exact effect sizes, the PENS psychometrics and the live Quantic strategy profile weren't retrieved. The "7,500 players" figure was reported by a secondary source and is unverified, so omitted.
